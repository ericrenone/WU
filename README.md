### I.1 Thought Experiment: The Mirror That Decays Asymmetrically

Before 1956, the consensus of particle physics was that the laws of nature are mirror-symmetric. If you filmed the beta decay of a nucleus and then showed the film to its mirror image, the two should be indistinguishable. More precisely: the rate at which a spinning nucleus emits beta particles in the direction of its spin should equal the rate in the opposite direction. The physical world and its mirror image obey the same laws; there is no absolute "left" and "right" in the universe.

Hold a cobalt-60 nucleus in a strong magnetic field that aligns its spin. At very low temperature — near 0.01 K — the thermal fluctuations are small enough to maintain the alignment. Now watch the beta electrons emitted by the nuclear decay.

If the mirror symmetry holds, the electrons should emerge in equal numbers pointing parallel to the spin and anti-parallel to the spin. The distribution of emitted electrons should be uniform, like a sphere. The mirror image of the spinning nucleus, emitting electrons symmetrically, would look identical to the original.

In 1956–57, Chien-Shiung Wu, working at Columbia University and the National Bureau of Standards with the cryogenics team of Ernest Ambler, Ray Hayward, Dale Hoppes, and Ralph Hudson, observed something else. The electrons are emitted preferentially in the direction opposite to the nuclear spin. The distribution is not a sphere; it is a lopsided ovoid. The mirror image — in which the electrons emerge preferentially in the same direction as the spin, because the mirror reverses the spatial coordinate but not the spin — does not match the original. The mirror image of the cobalt-60 decay does not occur in nature as observed.

Mirror symmetry P is violated. The universe distinguishes left from right at the most fundamental level of the weak interaction.

## Part II · The Meitner–Curie Axis: The Nuclear Time Arrow

### II.1 Thought Experiment: The Nucleus That Cannot Unknow

December 1938. A snowy forest in Sweden. Lise Meitner, recently exiled from Nazi Germany with barely ten marks and a diamond ring given her by Otto Hahn, sits on a log with her nephew Otto Frisch. Hahn has written from Berlin: his experiments have produced barium in uranium samples bombarded with neutrons. Barium has atomic number 56. Uranium has atomic number 92. Something has produced an element less than half the atomic number of the original. No known nuclear reaction can do this.

Meitner picks up a scrap of paper. She knows Bohr's liquid-drop model of the nucleus. A very heavy, unstable nucleus, she reasons, might elongate under the neutron's energy, stretch, neck down, and split — like a liquid drop dividing into two smaller drops. She calculates the masses of the likely fragments. The two daughter nuclei will have a combined mass slightly less than the original nucleus — a deficit of about one-fifth of a proton mass, corresponding to roughly 200 MeV via E = mc². The surface-tension energy of the single large drop exceeds the combined surface-tension energy of the two smaller drops; the difference is released as kinetic energy. The fragments fly apart at high speed. The reaction is exothermic. It is nuclear fission.

Now ask: can the reverse happen? Can the two daughter nuclei — barium and krypton, flying apart at the speed of fast neutrons — spontaneously collide, fuse, and reassemble into a uranium nucleus? The answer is: not under any physically accessible condition. The kinetic energy of the fragments is $200$ MeV per event — eight orders of magnitude above the thermal energies available in any ordinary matter. The fragments have diverged irreversibly. The mass has converted to energy and the energy has dispersed. The nuclear fission arrow points in one direction only.

This is the nuclear time arrow at the scale of individual nuclei. Not the statistical arrow of the second law, which says entropy increases on average in large systems, but a single-event, deterministic arrow: the fission of uranium is irreversible not because it is statistically unlikely to run backward but because no mechanism exists by which 200 MeV of kinetic energy dispersed across two nuclear fragments flying apart at ~$3 \times 10^7$ m/s can be concentrated back into a single nuclear event under the conditions of the known universe.

### II.3 Curie's Radioactive Decay Standard

Marie Curie (Warsaw 1867 — Sancellemoz 1934) measured radioactivity using the piezoelectric quartz electrometer designed by her husband Pierre Curie and his brother Jacques (PIEZO framework). The radioactive sample ionised air in a chamber; the resulting ionisation current — of order $10^{-13}$ amperes — was balanced against a known charge generated by hanging a calibrated weight from a piezoelectric quartz strip. The null-balance condition — electrometer needle stationary — gave the radioactive current with picocoulomb precision.

The radioactive decay constant $\lambda = \ln 2 / t_{1/2}$, where $t_{1/2}$ is the half-life, encodes the nucleus's probability of transformation per unit time. Curie discovered that the activity — the decay rate — falls exponentially: $A(t) = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$. This exponential is the nuclear time arrow in its most operational form: given the activity $A(t)$ and the decay constant $\lambda$, one can determine the age $t = -\ln(A/A_0)/\lambda$ with a precision limited only by the measurement of $A$ and the knowledge of $\lambda$.

Radiocarbon dating (Libby 1949) is the direct descendant of Curie's radioactive time arrow: the $^{14}$C decay constant $\lambda = 1.21 \times 10^{-4}$ yr$^{-1}$ provides a time standard from ~100 to ~50,000 years in the past. Uranium-lead geochronology extends the arrow to $\sim 4.5$ billion years. The thorium-229 nuclear clock (2026) extends the arrow to the most precise measurement of frequency achievable by any nuclear transition — the direct deployment of Curie's radioactive time arrow in its most precision-engineered form.

## Part III · The Goeppert Mayer Axis: Magic Numbers as Temporal Scaffolding

### III.1 Thought Experiment: The Shell That Refuses to Vibrate

Consider two nuclei: tin-120 ($^{120}$Sn, Z=50, N=70) and lead-208 ($^{208}$Pb, Z=82, N=126). Both sit at or near magic-number occupancies. Both are extraordinarily stable: lead-208 is doubly magic (Z=82, N=126) and is the heaviest stable nuclide; tin has the largest number of stable isotopes of any element (ten), reflecting its magic proton number Z=50.

Now perturb the nucleus. Strike it with a photon, add a nucleon, or heat the surrounding plasma. An ordinary nucleus would respond readily: its nucleons would rearrange into excited states, the nucleus would deform, vibrate, and either return to its ground state by photon emission or transition to a new configuration. The magic-number nucleus is different. Its outermost nucleon shell is completely filled; there are no nearby excited states into which the outer nucleons can be promoted without crossing a large energy gap. The nucleus resists deformation. It has an anomalously low first excited state, a high binding energy per nucleon near the magic number, and an enhanced stability against spontaneous fission.

This is the magic-number scaffold: the nuclear-physics analogue of the closed-shell inertness of noble gases in atomic physics. Where neon (Z=8, N=8) and argon (Z=18, N=18) are chemically inert because their electron shells are filled, $^{208}$Pb and other doubly-magic nuclei are temporally inert because their nucleon shells are filled. They resist the nuclear time arrow — they decay more slowly, survive longer in stellar interiors, and are over-represented in the cosmic abundance of elements.

### III.2 Goeppert Mayer's Shell Model and the Spin-Orbit Key

Maria Goeppert Mayer (Kattowitz 1906 — San Diego 1972), working at the University of Chicago Institute for Nuclear Studies and Argonne National Laboratory from 1946, approached nuclear physics without the theoretical preconceptions that had biased her contemporaries toward the liquid-drop model. Examining the known isotope abundances, she noticed that elements with proton numbers or neutron numbers equal to 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, or 126 appeared with anomalously high frequency — they were cosmically over-abundant, they had more stable isotopes than their neighbours, and they exhibited anomalously large binding energies.

The first three magic numbers (2, 8, 20) had been explained by a simple harmonic potential model for nucleon motion in the nuclear mean field. But the higher magic numbers (28, 50, 82, 126) could not be reproduced. In her second paper (Physical Review 75, 1969, June 1949), Goeppert Mayer identified the missing ingredient: strong spin-orbit coupling between each nucleon's orbital angular momentum $\vec{l}$ and its spin angular momentum $\vec{s}$. The spin-orbit term $V_{so} \propto \vec{l} \cdot \vec{s}$ splits each orbital level into two sub-levels (for $j = l + 1/2$ and $j = l - 1/2$), dramatically reordering the level sequence and producing the complete sequence of magic numbers. She communicated this insight to Enrico Fermi during a brief conversation; he is reported to have asked "Is there any evidence for spin-orbit coupling?" She replied: "The magic numbers." He said: "That's a good hint." That conversation established the nuclear shell model.

The WU framework's identification: the magic numbers are the nuclear-physics realisation of the temporal-stability scaffold. The thorium-229 nucleus has 90 protons and 139 neutrons. The nuclear clock is therefore simultaneously a precision time standard and a dark-matter detector.
